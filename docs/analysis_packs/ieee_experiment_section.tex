\section{Case Studies}
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
The case study is performed on the IEEE 33-node radial distribution feeder. The
feeder contains critical and non-critical load buses, and EV charging stations
can be placed at candidate buses along the main feeder and laterals. Each
opened station may install slow and fast EVSEs, with the station-level upper
bounds given in Table~\ref{tab:setup_parameters}. The normal-operation stage
models 24 hours of EV charging service and distribution-system operation. The
disaster stage models the 10:00--14:00 emergency window, in which available EV
discharging capability is used to support loads after line outages.

Table~\ref{tab:setup_parameters} lists the parameters used in the default
study. The default uncertainty support contains ten normal scenarios and ten
disaster operating scenarios. The outage ambiguity set uses budget $K=2$, so
the adversarial replay can select two failed lines when evaluating disaster
resilience. The reported objective components are always computed after the
first-stage siting and sizing decisions are fixed and replayed under this
common evaluator.

\subsection{Scenario Generation and Uncertainty Support}
The normal scenarios $a\in A$ contain the time-varying load and regional EV
charging-demand profiles. The disaster scenarios $b\in B$ contain the
post-disaster load and available V2G discharging profiles. The line-outage
uncertainty is not treated as another sampled scenario. Instead, the outage
pattern is chosen by the DRO separation problem subject to the budget $K$.
Thus $B$ describes disaster operating conditions, while $K$ controls the
adversarial line-outage support.

Figs.~\ref{fig:normal_load_profile}--\ref{fig:disaster_ev_profile} show
representative profiles from the same audited $10\times 10$ data source used
in the default optimization and replay. The slow-charging demand has a broad
daily profile, whereas fast charging is more concentrated in short peaks. The
disaster V2G profiles are defined on the four-period emergency window, which
creates a different operating tradeoff from ordinary charging service.

\subsection{Benchmark Definitions}
Four cases are compared. Case~1 is the proposed integrated DRO planner, which
jointly optimizes annualized construction cost, normal-operation recourse, and
disaster-stage DRO recourse. Case~2 is the normal-operation-only benchmark,
which removes the disaster term and therefore represents the daily-service
endpoint. Case~3 is the disaster-resilience-only benchmark, which removes the
normal-stage EV service objective and constraints during training; the resulting
plan is then replayed under the normal evaluator to show the daily-service
consequence of a disaster-only design. Case~4 is a fair deterministic
mean-value benchmark. It replaces the multi-scenario normal and disaster
supports by their mean profiles, but solves the same integrated planning model
with the same outage budget, $K_{\rm train}=2$.

All four first-stage plans are frozen and evaluated under the same
$|A|=10$, $|B|=10$, $K_{\rm eval}=2$ MILP worst-distribution evaluator. Hence
the comparisons below are not comparisons of four different training
objectives. They are common-scale ex-post comparisons of the resulting plans.
The training objective for the accepted default uses normalized top-level
multipliers
$(m_{\rm cons},m_{\rm nor},m_{\rm dis})=(0.016,1,1.4)$, while all reported
costs in the tables are raw physical/economic components without these
multipliers.

Case~2 demonstrates the normal-service endpoint. It opens only five stations
and achieves nearly the same normal component as the proposed plan:
$\Psi^{\rm nor}$ is 62,280.31 in Case~2 and 62,324.28 in Case~1. This small
normal-operation difference is expected because both plans preserve low
transportation cost and zero unmet charging demand. The difference appears
when the plans are replayed under disaster uncertainty. Case~2 covers only
1/11 critical buses directly, and its $\Phi^{\rm dis}$ is 16,844.36. The
integrated plan increases construction cost and opens broader feeder coverage,
but reduces $\Phi^{\rm dis}$ to 5,223.70, a 68.99\% reduction.

Case~3 gives the opposite endpoint. It opens eleven stations, directly covers
8/11 critical buses, and has the lowest disaster component,
$\Phi^{\rm dis}=4,557.02$. This confirms that the disaster-only benchmark is
indeed the most resilience-oriented design. However, that design is not a good
daily-service plan. Under the same normal replay, $F^{\rm unmet}$ rises to
137,689.83 and accounts for 68.36\% of $\Psi^{\rm nor}$. The integrated plan
therefore sits between the two partial frameworks: it keeps zero unmet demand
like the normal-only endpoint, while recovering most of the disaster benefit of
the disaster-only endpoint.

The topology in Fig.~\ref{fig:default_maps} explains this tradeoff. Case~1
keeps daily-service anchors at buses 3, 6, 30, and 32, but adds buses 8, 11,
14, 17, 18, 21, 26, and 29 relative to Case~2 while dropping bus 19. These
additional stations spread capacity along the main radial backbone and the two
laterals. In physical terms, the integrated model does not simply add chargers
at the cheapest daily-demand locations. It shifts siting toward critical-bus
neighborhoods and the far upper lateral, increasing direct critical-bus
coverage from 1/11 to 7/11. This is the mechanism behind the component table:
the plan pays for wider first-stage coverage, not for a large change in daily
EV transportation service.

\subsection{Deterministic Worst-Distribution Comparison}

outage budget itself. The deterministic plan is trained on mean normal and
mean disaster profiles, but it still uses $K_{\rm train}=2$. Table

replays both under the same $|A|=10$, $|B|=10$, $K_{\rm eval}=2$ evaluator.

The deterministic solution is a meaningful comparator because it has the same
outage-budget semantics as the proposed model. Its weakness is that the mean
profiles do not represent the multi-scenario tails used in the common replay.
It opens eleven stations and installs 171 slow and 15 fast EVSEs, whereas the
proposed DRO plan opens twelve stations and installs 172 slow and 15 fast
EVSEs. The two plans have similar aggregate charger counts, but they place that
capacity differently. Case~1 expands direct critical-bus coverage from 2/11 to
7/11 by selecting buses 11, 14, 17, 26, and 29 that the mean-value plan does
not open. Under the same worst-distribution replay, this broader critical-bus
topology reduces $\Phi^{\rm dis}$ from 7,032.12 to 5,223.70, which is a
25.72\% reduction. The comparison therefore isolates the robustness value of
multi-scenario DRO: Case~1 pays a larger first-stage construction component but
obtains lower worst-distribution disaster cost while retaining zero unmet
charging demand.

\subsection{Certification and Reproducibility}
Table~\ref{tab:certification} reports the certification status for the four
default runs. The normal-only benchmark is solved directly to optimality. The
three DRO/Benders runs reach the $\epsilon$-certified stopping rule under the
same $|A|=10$, $|B|=10$, $K=2$ support. For traceability, the component rows,
plans, replay logs, and figure sources are retained with the experiment pack;
rerunning the recorded instance reproduces all entries in
Table~\ref{tab:default_components}, including station counts, charger counts,
critical-bus coverage values, and open-bus sets.

The resulting default case supports the main claim of this section: compared
with normal-only planning, the integrated DRO model purchases broader critical
coverage with almost no normal-service degradation; compared with
disaster-only planning, it avoids the large unmet-demand penalty; and compared
with a fair mean-value deterministic planner using the same outage budget, it
achieves lower worst-distribution disaster cost under the common replay.
